About Me!

My name is Nora Helen Cojocea-Neagoie. I'm in the process of receiving my Bachelor of Science in Aerospace Engineering at the University of Virginia (Class of 2029, GPA 3.55). My path started with NASA's Virginia Aerospace Science and Technology Scholars program, where I worked as a Risk and Radiation Specialist on a hypothetical crewed Mars mission — and it's kept pulling me toward the intersection of physical systems and the data that describes them.

Since then I've mentored fellow scholars for NASA's Virginia Space Grant Consortium, built a machine learning model to study satellite propellant consumption at Iridium Communications, and led a 12-person team through the design of a conceptual ice-sheet-tracking satellite. Outside of internships, I'm on the Research and Design team for UVA's Rocketry Club, working on the oxidizer system for a K-class hybrid rocket engine.

Projects

Two problems, two very different fixes

01

Adaptive Landing Gear for VTOL Aircraft

Independent research — Virginia State & Regional Science and Engineering Fair, 2024–2025

Helicopters and drones that land on slopes or debris risk dynamic rollover or propeller strikes — a real problem for search-and-rescue, disaster relief, and urban air mobility, where a flat pad is never guaranteed. Most existing fixes rely on LiDAR or mid-air adjustment, both of which fail when sensors get noisy or the ground shifts after touchdown.

I designed a passive, mechanical landing gear that locks on impact rather than adjusting mid-flight, modeled in SolidWorks after the push-button locking mechanism in umbrellas. I ran CFD tests in SimScale to confirm the legs wouldn't disturb the aircraft's aerodynamics, 3D-printed the assembly, then wrote the Arduino-based control logic that decides when to lock the legs. The system needs no LiDAR or IMU — just a single camera — and was flight-tested both indoors and outdoors.

02

Analyze Sports: Data Visualization

Built for Computers for Kids, a data-science nonprofit for ages 11–14

Computers for Kids runs a tabletop-basketball activity to teach data science to younger students, but logging every shot through a manual Google Form interrupted gameplay and put up a barrier for less tech-savvy players.

Our team replaced it with a browser-based app, built in Django and Python, that logs each make or miss by court zone in real time and renders a live shooting-percentage heatmap as the game is played — turning data collection into part of the activity instead of a pause in it.
